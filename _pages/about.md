<h3 class="ucla-heading">Namaste/Salaam!</h3>

I am a PhD Candidate in Political Science at the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://polisci.ucla.edu/), where I am privileged to be advised by [Daniel Posner](https://danielnposner.com/). Before joining UCLA, I earned an MSc (2020) with Distinction in Comparative Politics from the [London School of Economics and Political Science](https://www.lse.ac.uk/), and a BSc (2019) with Honours in Economics from the [Symbiosis School of Economics](https://www.sse.ac.in/).

My research draws on modern computational methods and social psychological experiments to study mass politics and intergroup conflict in South Asia and other contexts. You can find my CV [here]({{ '/assets/pdf/202608CV.pdf' | relative_url }}).


<h3 class="ucla-heading">Dissertation</h3>

My dissertation comprises three empirical chapters. 

My dissertation examines how political campaigns shape electoral outcomes, social identities, and intergroup conflict in India. Across three empirical chapters, I combine original data collection, quasi-experimental research designs, and survey experiments to study how mass political mobilization affects citizens both inside and outside the voting booth.

In my job market paper, I study whether high-profile campaign appearances actually win votes. I construct a new dataset documenting geospatial data on each political rally, public meeting, and other campaign appearances by six leading politicians from India’s two largest national parties across every national and state election between 2014-2026. Because parties strategically choose where their leaders campaign, simple comparisons between visited and unvisited constituencies are unlikely to identify causal effects. To address this problem, I exploit scheduled appearances that were unexpectedly cancelled because of idiosyncratic disruptions such as mechanical failures and acute personal emergencies. Comparing realized and unexpectedly cancelled appearances allows me to estimate the electoral return to a leader actually arriving in a constituency that the campaign had already chosen to target.

The second chapter examines how political campaigns activate social identities and behavior, including along dimensions that campaigns do not explicitly invoke. I develop the idea that political appeals activate not only identity categories but also the norms, practices, and social meanings encoded within them. Focusing on Hindu nationalism and caste, I investigate whether political mobilization around a common Hindu identity has downstream effects on caste identities, caste boundaries, and caste-related behavior. I combine survey-experimental evidence with observational evidence from historical episodes of Hindu nationalist mobilization to study how campaigns organized around one social cleavage can generate consequences for another.

The third chapter asks whether political campaigns can affect intergroup conflict. I develop a cheap, auditable, and replicable computational pipeline for identifying, extracting, and geolocating events from newspaper archives, which I use to construct a novel dataset documenting Hindu–Muslim communal mobilization and violence in India between 2017 and 2024. I then use these data to examine the Bharat Jodo Yatra (Unite India March), a 150-day political campaign organized around national unity, common purpose, and interfaith solidarity. After geolocating the march route, I estimate whether communities exposed to the campaign experienced subsequent reductions in Hindu–Muslim violence. I find little evidence of substantial reductions in conflict. I conclude by considering the reach and limits of political campaigns as instruments for changing social behavior and intergroup relations.

Together, the three chapters examine what political campaigns can---and cannot---do, providing a broader account of how electoral campaigns reshape political and social life in divided democracies.
